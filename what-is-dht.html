What Is DHT, and Why Does It Cause Hair Loss?

6 min read · By The Crown Report Research Desk · we may earn a commission from marked partner links (details)

The short version: DHT is a potent androgen your body makes from testosterone using an enzyme called 5-alpha-reductase. In follicles that inherited sensitivity to it, DHT shortens each growth cycle until hairs come back thinner, shorter, and eventually not at all. Every drug that actually stops hair loss works by lowering DHT. Nothing you can buy off a shelf touches it.

Where DHT comes from

Testosterone circulates; an enzyme called 5-alpha-reductase converts a portion of it into dihydrotestosterone, which binds androgen receptors far more strongly than testosterone does. The enzyme has two main forms, type 1 and type 2, which matters later when we talk about drugs.

DHT is not a villain in general. It drives normal male development and it is why the same hormone that thins scalp hair thickens beard and body hair. Follicles differ in what they do with the same signal.

What it does to a follicle

In genetically sensitive follicles, DHT progressively shortens the growth phase of the hair cycle. Each cycle produces a hair that is a little finer, a little shorter, a little lighter. That process is called miniaturisation, and it is why hair loss looks like thinning long before it looks like baldness: the follicles are still there, producing worse and worse hair, until eventually they produce none.

The inherited part is receptor sensitivity, not DHT levels. Two men with identical hormone panels can have completely different hairlines at forty. This is also why the shape is predictable: temples and crown carry the sensitive follicles, the sides and back generally do not, which is why the horseshoe pattern exists and why transplanted donor hair keeps growing.

How the drugs interrupt it

Finasteride blocks type 2 of the enzyme and lowers serum DHT by roughly 70%. Dutasteride blocks both types and lowers it by roughly 90%, which is why it is the escalation option; the head-to-head data is in dutasteride vs finasteride. Topical versions aim the same mechanism at the scalp with much lower blood levels, covered in the topical finasteride guide.

Minoxidil does something different: it does not touch DHT at all, it pushes resting follicles back into growth. That is why the two are paired. One removes the cause, the other restores the cycle.

What does not block DHT

Caffeine shampoo sits on your scalp for ninety seconds; DHT works from the bloodstream around the clock. Biotin addresses a deficiency you probably do not have, covered in does biotin work. Saw palmetto has weak, inconsistent evidence and no dosing standard. Rosemary oil has one interesting trial, discussed in rosemary oil for hair growth. None of these is a DHT blocker in the way the prescription drugs are, and the marketing that implies otherwise is the most profitable sentence in the category.

Why this matters for your plan

Once you understand that the cause is a hormone acting on inherited receptors, the treatment logic writes itself: lower the hormone, restart the cycle, start before the follicles scar. That is the entire content of the prevention guide, and the budget guide prices it from ten dollars a month.
