The Norwood Scale, Stage by Stage

6 min read · By The Crown Report Research Desk · we may earn a commission from marked partner links (details)

Quick answer

The Norwood scale runs from stage 1, no visible loss, to stage 7, a bare crown with a horseshoe rim. Photograph your hairline and crown in flat light to place yourself, because the stage you start treating at is roughly the stage you keep.

The short version: the Norwood scale is the seven-stage map doctors use to describe male pattern baldness, from a full head at stage 1 to a horseshoe at stage 7. It is useful for one reason: it tells you how much you still have to protect. Treatment preserves living follicles, so the stage you start at is roughly the stage you keep.

The stages, in plain English

Stage 1. No visible recession. The hairline you had at fifteen.

Stage 2. Slight recession at the temples, the mature hairline most men reach in their twenties. Not everyone at stage 2 progresses; some stop here for life.

Stage 3. The first stage classified as balding. Temples deepen into recognisable corners. Stage 3 vertex adds visible thinning at the crown.

Stage 4. Corners deepen further and the crown thinning becomes obvious, with a band of hair still separating the two.

Stage 5. That separating band narrows and thins.

Stage 6. The band is gone; front and crown have joined into one bald region.

Stage 7. Only the horseshoe of hair around the sides and back remains, the DHT-resistant zone that is also the donor area for transplants.

How to place yourself honestly

Not in a hotel bathroom under downlights, which flatten and exaggerate everything. Natural indirect light, dry hair, three photos: hairline straight on, crown from above, and one three-quarter angle. Compare against the same three shots a month later; a single snapshot tells you almost nothing about direction of travel. Our photo comparer puts two dates side by side.

Why the stage decides your plan

Every treatment we cover protects follicles that still function and revives ones that are shrinking. It cannot resurrect follicles that scarred over years ago. So stage 2 to 3 is where medication delivers its best results: there is a lot to protect and the miniaturised hairs can still thicken back up. By stage 5 or 6, medication defends what is left but the bare regions are a surgical conversation, which we cover in transplant vs medication.

That is the whole argument for acting early, stated without drama: the earlier stage you start treating at, the better the hair you get to keep.
